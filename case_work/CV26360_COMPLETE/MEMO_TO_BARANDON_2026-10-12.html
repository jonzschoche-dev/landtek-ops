C10. "Builders in good faith": how I understand the defense comes apart

Answer ¶49 invokes Articles 448, 546 and 548 and asks for the price, the real property taxes and arrears, the registration and transfer expenses, the survey expenses and the value of necessary and useful improvements, with retention until paid. Mrs. Balane's excluded affidavit puts the improvements at "at least" ₱100,000. On my reading the defense fails in each of three periods, for different reasons, and I would be grateful for your view.

Before the deed (1975 to September 2016). By their own Answer (¶¶40, 43, 44[b]) and all three of their affidavits, the family held the lot as tenants of the owners' administrator. The Supreme Court has held that Article 448, with Article 546, "applies only to a possessor in good faith, i.e., one who builds on land with the belief that he is the owner thereof," and "does not apply where one's only interest is that of a lessee under a rental contract" (Parilla v. Pilar, G.R. No. 167680, 30 November 2006). A lessee's improvements fall under Article 1678: at the end of the lease the lessor chooses between paying one-half of their value and letting the lessee remove them, and there is no right of retention. Whatever they built while renting is therefore an Article 1678 matter, at the owners' option, and it is for them to prove which structures date from this period and what they are worth.

From the deed to registration (September 2016 to November 2021). They plead ownership by purchase, not tolerance. Judged as buyers, they bought from an agent outside his written authority and never asked the owners (C3, C8); a buyer who fails that inquiry is not in good faith from the day of the deed, and good faith must in any case last until registration (Duenas v. Metrobank, G.R. No. 209463, En Banc, 29 November 2022). A builder who is not in good faith "loses what is built ... without right to indemnity" (Article 449); the owner may demand demolition at the builder's expense (Article 450) and damages (Article 451). Only necessary expenses are refundable (Article 546, first paragraph), and only a possessor in good faith may retain the thing until reimbursed. Their 2006 survey receipt, in their own Exhibit 5, says the lot was "owned by the Heirs of Worrick Keesey"; in the year they paid to carve out their lot they wrote down whose land it was.

After the demand (received 22 August 2025) and the summons. Good faith, if any, is lost "from the moment facts exist showing that the possessor is not unaware that he possesses the thing improperly" (Article 528). From that date all fruits are owed, including the room rentals their Answer admits (¶27), under Article 549, and anything built or rebuilt is forfeit.

What they will cite. Macasaet v. Macasaet, G.R. Nos. 154391-92 (30 September 2004) and Department of Education v. Casibang, G.R. No. 192268 (27 January 2016), which applied Article 448 to people who built with the landowner's own invitation or permission. No owner here consented to any structure; the only relationship was a lease, which Parilla puts under Article 1678; and ¶49 rests on the deed, not on tolerance.

The reimbursement list, item by item. The price: no co-owner received a peso (C6); the claim lies against Cesar's estate, which their own ¶38 calls the party whose "acts, receipts, collections" are "the very foundation" of the case. Capital gains tax, documentary stamp tax, transfer tax with surcharge and interest, and registration fees: expenses of registering a void sale, not expenses necessary to preserve the land. The survey: an expense of the same transaction, incurred to cut the owners' land without their consent. Real property taxes actually paid on the land (¶44[i]): the one necessary expense, refundable on proof by official receipts, without retention. Improvements: Article 1678 before the deed, Article 449 after it. The houses of the other families on the lot are not their improvements at all.

C12. The statutes, in plain words: what each side will cite and where I think the court lands

Annex 4 is a full section-by-section map of the provisions each side will put before the court, with the exact words and the exhibit that fits each. Because the judge decides by the text of the section and not by case law (C11), I have tried to set out here, in plain words, the ten collisions and the one word in each that I think decides it.

  1. Can this court cancel the title? They say attacking a title "collaterally" is forbidden (PD 1529, Section 48). But Section 48 forbids only attacks made in passing in some other case; it allows cancellation "in a direct proceeding." Our Complaint prays for the cancellation of TCT No. 079-2021002126 in its caption, which the judge herself recited in her first order. This is the direct proceeding. The word that decides it: direct.
  2. What could Cesar do? Both sides cite Article 1874: a sale of land through an agent needs written authority, "otherwise, the sale shall be void." They say the 1992 SPA is the writing. It is, and the writing itself limits the sale to people holding Llamanzares contracts to sell or who paid Llamanzares, and only after consulting the owners. The judge will read the writing. The word: writing.
  3. Was the Balane sale inside that writing? They will cite Articles 1900 and 1902: a buyer may rely on the power of attorney "as written" and is not bound by the owners' "private or secret" instructions. Those articles help us: the class and the consultation are not secret instructions, they are the written terms, and they were on the title from 1996. Article 1898 then makes the sale void where the buyer "is aware of the limits," and the registry is what made her aware. They will also cite Article 1911 (the owners "allowed" Cesar to act with full powers). Our answer: from August 2005 the owners' conduct was a written revocation, and Article 1911 creates liability, not a valid transfer of land. The word: written.
  4. Void, or merely fixable? This is their sixth defense: Article 1403 makes an unauthorized contract "unenforceable, unless ratified," and they say the estate ratified by taking the money. Article 1874, the special rule for land sold through an agent, says "void," and Article 1409 says contracts "declared void by law … cannot be ratified." Void beats unenforceable because it is the specific rule, and nothing void can be ratified. The money went to a revoked agent and his son and never reached any owner anyway. The word: void.
  5. Does the revocation, or its non-registration, matter? They cite Article 1921 (revocation does not prejudice the "specified persons" the agent was to deal with unless they were notified) and PD 1529, Section 64 (a revocation "shall be registered"). Article 1921 protects only the specified persons, and the SPA names them; Mrs. Balane was never one, so the revocation is beside the point for her. Section 64 is a direction to register, not a condition of validity, and it only matters for a sale inside the power. And what was registered in 1996 was the SPA itself, limits included (Section 52). The one thing we must not do is argue that the 2020 newspaper notice was Article 1922 "sufficient warning" while also arguing that Article 1922 does not apply; it does not apply, because Cesar's powers were special, not general. The word: specified.
  6. Was she a buyer in good faith, and until when did she have to be? They cite Article 527 (good faith is presumed) and Section 53 (an "innocent holder for value" is protected). Article 526 defines good faith: not "aware" of any flaw in the mode of acquisition. Article 528 says good faith ends "from the moment facts exist" showing awareness. Section 51 makes registration the "operative act," and the Supreme Court reads that to mean good faith must last until registration, which here was 23 November 2021. The facts in that window are dated and on paper: her own 2020 power of attorney to sue over the land, the pleading with the newspaper notice received by her side's lawyer in July 2021, her processor's certified copy of the SPA in August 2021, and her sworn "my property" in September 2021. The judge does not have to call anyone dishonest; she has to find that facts existed. The word: aware.
  7. Could a specific lot ever be titled to her? Article 496: partition is made "by agreement between the parties," and the parties to a partition of co-owned land are the co-owners. No co-owner signed the 2017 partition; no power of attorney ever allowed Cesar to partition; and the partition cites a survey plan that did not exist until 2020. They have no article for this. Even if the court upheld the deed, the title falls here. The word: parties.
  8. What about the houses? They cite Articles 448 and 546: a builder "in good faith" is paid for improvements and may stay until paid. Both articles turn on the words "in good faith," which Article 526 defines. Before the deed they were renters by their own Answer, and a renter's improvements fall under Article 1678 (the owner pays half their value or lets the renter remove them; no right to stay). After the deed they were buyers who never asked the owners, and a builder not in good faith "loses what is built … without right to indemnity" (Article 449) and may be made to demolish at his expense (Article 450); only expenses of preservation are refunded (Article 452). The word: renting, in their own Answer.
  9. Rents and damages. They cite Article 544 (a good-faith possessor keeps the fruits). Article 549 makes a bad-faith possessor return them, and their Answer admits rooms were rented. Exemplary damages need a finding that they acted in a "fraudulent" or "oppressive" manner (Article 2232); this judge will not make it on an impression, so we prove it by five dated documents: the deed's "in hand paid" against receipts to February 2017; the 2016 petition that hid the sale and listed a title cancelled in 1999; the 2017 partition under a 2020 plan; the 2020 survey certified with the "consent of the owners" when the only agent was dead; the 2021 "my property" over land titled to three other people. Attorney's fees follow (Article 2208). The word: fraudulent, proved by dates.
  10. Their counterclaim. It rests on the suit being malicious. A suit that succeeds on title cannot have been malicious, and the agency referrals they call harassment are the agencies' own acts. The counterclaim stands or falls with the title (Rule 6, Section 7).

Three words carry the case on her method: void (Article 1874), aware (Article 526) and parties (Article 496). A fourth sits inside the class clause itself: therefor (a down payment is paid for a sale, and a sale needs an object and a price; C13). Each is on the page of an exhibit already in the record. C13. The class clause, word by word, and what a "downpayment" requires

Paragraph 7 of the SPA reads: "in favor of third persons in possession of CONTRACTS TO SELL executed by the previous administrator, MR. BEN LLAMANZARES and/or to those who have paid their downpayments or have made partial payments therefor to the latter." As I read it, and as I will answer if asked:

  1. Two sub-classes, both tied to one named man. Holders of a written contract to sell from Llamanzares; and persons who had paid Llamanzares a down payment or partial payment. "In possession of" takes "CONTRACTS TO SELL" as its object, which is what the next words say Llamanzares "executed"; reading it as possession of the land requires deleting the words that follow. "Therefor" means for the sale, so rent is not a payment under the clause; their own receipts say "Payment for the month of." "The latter" can only be Llamanzares, so every receipt signed by Cesar's son, the earliest in October 2003, is outside it. "Have paid … have made" closes the class on 16 March 1992. Paragraph 6's "interested third parties" is the purpose; paragraph 7 begins "Accordingly" and is the operative grant.
  2. A "downpayment" presupposes a sale with an object and a price. "There is no contract unless … consent … object certain … [and] cause" concur (Art. 1318). A sale requires "a determinate thing" and "a price certain" (Art. 1458); the thing must be "particularly designated or physically segregated from all others of the same class" (Art. 1460); the sale exists only on "a meeting of minds upon the thing … and upon the price" (Art. 1475); earnest money is "part of the price and … proof of the perfection of the contract" (Art. 1482). So the second sub-class is the same people as the first at an earlier documentary stage: persons who had agreed with Llamanzares on an identified portion and a price and had begun paying. On the defendants' own evidence no object or price existed in Llamanzares's time: the portion was first segregated by the survey they paid for in December 2006 (their Exhibit 5, page 1, "lot owned by the Heirs of Worrick Keesey"); "Lot Area: 2,587" first appears on a receipt of 7 August 2016; and the price of ₱250,000 was fixed by Cesar (Mrs. Balane's excluded affidavit, S33). A ₱3,000 payment in the 1980s with no lot, no price and no receipt is money handed to the administrator, which on their own Answer is what rent was.
  3. Whose burden. Membership is their fourth affirmative defense (¶40) and their first three good-faith circumstances (¶44[a] to [c]); an affirmative allegation is proved by the party making it (Rule 131, Sec. 1), and the buyer claiming good faith carries the burden anyway. They must produce a Llamanzares contract or an agreed portion and price with Llamanzares before 16 March 1992. They have produced neither.
  4. What they have instead. Three accounts that do not match: ₱3,000 in the 1980s, no receipt, recorded in Manuel Violeta's "brown columnar book" said to have gone "to Cesar and then the Agcaoili Law Office" (Mrs. Balane, S20 to S24, excluded); "already paid" by about 1992 (Salvador, S14 to S15); and the only receipt marked "Down payment," ₱20,000 on 6 January 2005 to Salvador. Nothing shows the pleaded ₱3,000 "credited" in the 2016 price. Two things I will run down: whether the Agcaoili office ever received any Violeta ledger (if it did and it names a Balane, they gain a document; if it did not, her sworn account of where it went is wrong), and the Llamanzares-era sales recorded on the T-4497 title itself (the 1977 "Deeds of Final Sale" by "Mary Worrick-Keesey, thru her Atty.-in-fact Benjamin Llamanzares"), which show what a member of the class looked like: a registered instalment buyer with a registered deed.
  5. My mother's own sworn reading. Her affidavit of 22 February 2023 (¶4) describes the 1992 authority as "limited authority to perfect the sale of portions of said property in favor of those who already possessed Contract to Sell, or have had made down/partial payment to Benjamin Llamanzares." Salvador's 25 November 2025 letter says the same from the other side (C9).
  6. Cross questions this supports (for Salvador and Princess): which lot, at what price, agreed with whom, and when; where is the ₱3,000 credited; produce the Violeta book or say where it is.
